## Virtue Ethics

**Virtue ethics** focuses on the development of moral character and virtues as the foundation of ethical behavior.

Rather than emphasizing the consequences of actions, as in consequentialism, or adherence to moral duties, as in deontology, virtue ethics asks what kind of person we should become.

## Examples of Virtue Ethics

### Aristotle’s Virtue Ethics

Aristotle’s virtue ethics is grounded in the human function, which he identifies with reasoning.

### St. Benedict’s Ladder of Humility

St. Benedict’s Ladder of Humility offers a step-by-step approach to getting closer to God.

### Buddhist Virtue Ethics

Buddhist virtue ethics is an approach to coming to know the fundamental nature of the universe.

## Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana Buddhism emphasizes the **bodhisattva path**.

On this path, individuals delay their own enlightenment in order to help all beings achieve Buddhahood.

Mahayana Buddhism also incorporates distinctive philosophical concepts such as **emptiness**.

## The Six Perfections

## 1. Generosity

**Generosity** is the foundational virtue for cultivating compassion and ultimately achieving enlightenment.

The practice of generosity involves giving material goods and sharing the teachings of Buddhism.

Generosity in Buddhist virtue ethics goes beyond material giving.

It involves a mental shift in which one views the well-being of others as equal to one’s own.

### Case Study: Prince Vessantara

Prince Vessantara was renowned for his boundless compassion.

He gave away all of his possessions, including a magical elephant vital to his kingdom’s prosperity.

This act led to his banishment.

Even in exile, Vessantara continued to give. He offered his children as servants and his wife to a disguised deity.

Ultimately, he was reunited with his family and restored to power.

How would a Buddhist understanding of compassion explain Vessantara’s actions?

Did his extreme generosity serve a higher purpose, or did it risk harming those he cared for by neglecting their immediate needs?

## 2. Morality

**Morality** involves cultivating virtuous conduct through actions, speech, and thoughts.

The Buddhist moral code includes guidelines such as refraining from:

- Harming living creatures
- Stealing
- Lying
- Engaging in harmful speech

Adhering to moral precepts is seen as a foundation for developing compassion and wisdom.

These are essential for progressing on the path to enlightenment.

### Case Study: Sarah

Sarah, a manager, is tasked with delivering difficult news about budget cuts to her team.

Initially, she considers omitting key details to soften the impact and protect her image.

However, she decides to be fully transparent, sharing the challenges and offering support to help her team navigate the situation.

Her motivation shifts from simply following the rule of honesty to acting out of concern for her team’s well-being.

According to Buddhist ethics, which emphasizes compassion and reducing harm, is Sarah’s decision to be fully transparent the correct moral action, even if it risks discomfort or uncertainty?

## 3. Toleration

**Toleration** involves enduring suffering, patiently navigating interpersonal challenges, and accepting profound truths about reality.

Toleration in the face of adversity cultivates resilience and mental composure.

This allows an individual to remain steadfast on the path to enlightenment.

Ultimately, the practice of toleration, guided by wisdom and compassion, leads to greater understanding and skillful engagement with the world.

### Case Study: Maria

Maria, a community organizer advocating for racial equality, faces a protester who angrily opposes her cause and uses inflammatory language.

According to Buddhist teachings on toleration, how should Maria respond to this situation?

How can she balance her commitment to social justice with the practice of compassion and non-harm, while navigating the hostility of those who oppose her work?

Should her response prioritize patience and understanding, or is there a point where she must act more assertively in defense of her cause without losing sight of Buddhist principles?

## 4. Energy

**Energy** is the persistent effort required to cultivate wholesome qualities and overcome negative mental states.

The perfection of energy is characterized by:

- Enthusiasm
- Diligence
- Unwavering commitment to the path of enlightenment

This commitment continues even amidst challenges and setbacks.

Cultivating energy involves both physical and mental discipline.

A healthy body can support mental clarity and focus.

## 5. Meditation

**Meditation** includes a wide array of practices designed to cultivate mental discipline, insight, and ultimately enlightenment.

Buddhist meditation includes practices such as:

- Mindfulness of breathing
- Contemplation of the body’s impermanence
- Focused reflection on key Buddhist concepts

These practices aim to transform the practitioner’s understanding of reality.

They also cultivate qualities such as:

- Compassion
- Wisdom
- Equanimity

## 6. Wisdom

**Wisdom** is a deep understanding of reality, particularly the interconnectedness and impermanence of all phenomena.

Wisdom arises from meditation and contemplation on Buddhist teachings such as:

- Emptiness
- Dependent origination
- Not-self

Cultivating wisdom allows an individual to perceive the world clearly, free from delusion and attachment.

This is essential for acting skillfully and compassionately.

### Case Study: David

David, a community leader, is faced with a conflict between two local groups advocating for different solutions to a shared environmental problem.

Each group is deeply entrenched in its views, and tensions are rising.

David, rather than simply relying on his knowledge of the issue, reflects on the Buddhist concept of wisdom as understanding the interconnectedness of all things.

How would a Buddhist understanding of wisdom guide David in mediating this situation with compassion, clarity, and an open mind?
